Water Consumption by Data Centers

Why Do Data Centres Need Water?

Data centres operate non-stop and house thousands of servers that generate intense heat. If these servers are not cooled effectively, they risk overheating, which can lead to equipment damage, performance issues, or even full system shutdowns. Water is essential in many data centres because it provides an efficient and cost-effective method of heat removal. Most modern data centres use evaporative cooling systems, where warm air is passed through water-saturated pads or towers. As the water evaporates, it cools the air, which is then circulated back into the server rooms. This process is significantly more water-efficient than traditional cooling methods but still requires vast quantities of water daily. Key Fact: Some data centres use up to 300,000 gallons (1.1 million litres) of water per day just for cooling. That’s nearly enough to supply the daily water needs of 100,000 homes.

  

Increased Water Consumption Due To AI

The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) has made water consumption even more critical. AI workloads are far more compute-intensive than regular internet tasks, which leads to higher temperatures in data centre servers. As AI chips like GPUs work harder and hotter, they require more aggressive cooling, usually with water. For example, a user interacting with ChatGPT 20-50 times can lead to the indirect use of 500 millilitres of water (half a litre), primarily for cooling the servers that process the request.

  

Why Not Just Use Air?

Air cooling is an option, but it is often less efficient, especially in warmer climates. Using water allows data centres to maintain much more stable temperatures without the need to blast air conditioning continuously. Some large-scale operations, such as those in India and the southwestern US, prefer water cooling because it is cheaper in the long term and better at heat transfer. However, this efficiency comes at a cost: it significantly increases water demand, especially in regions already struggling with drought and water scarcity.

  

Scale of Water Use: Just How Much Water Do Data Centres Consume?

The water consumption of data centres is staggering, and is growing fast. With the boom in artificial intelligence (AI), cloud computing, and streaming services, demand for data processing is at an all-time high. And with it comes a surge in water use, particularly for cooling servers. In 2022, Google alone reported using 5.6 billion gallons (21.2 billion litres) of water across its data centres globally. This was a 20% increase from 2021. Microsoft consumed nearly 1.7 billion gallons (6.4 billion litres) of water globally in 2022, up 34% from the year before. Meta Platforms (Facebook) used around 2.2 billion gallons in the same year. When scaled to individual users, the impact might seem small, but collectively it becomes massive: If one user interacts with ChatGPT 20–50 times, he can cause the indirect use of half a litre (500ml) of water. Multiply that by millions of users daily, and it becomes hundreds of millions of litres per month.

  

USA vs. India

In water-stressed regions, the impact is even more severe.

  • USA: Many large data centres are built in arid regions like Arizona and Utah, where water is already scarce. For example, Google’s data centre in The Dalles, Oregon, was involved in a legal dispute over water rights, as it planned to consume over 1 million gallons per day.
  • India: India's data centre market is growing rapidly, with expected investments of $8 billion by 2026, much of which is going into hyperscale facilities. These centres rely heavily on chiller-based cooling, which uses substantial water in warmer cities like Mumbai and Hyderabad.
A single hyperscale data centre in India can use over 150,000 litres of water per day, mostly for cooling purposes.

  

Environmental Consequences of Excessive Water Use

  • Pressure on Local Water Resources
Data centres, especially those using evaporative cooling, consume enormous amounts of freshwater, often in direct competition with households, agriculture, and local ecosystems. In regions already facing droughts or water scarcity, data centres can aggravate the crisis. For example, Arizona and California, home to several massive cloud campuses are among the most water-stressed states in the U.S. In India, where water stress is growing, especially in urban regions like Delhi, Hyderabad, and Chennai, the demand from data centres could divert water away from public use, leading to conflicts and systemic water shortages.

  • Threats to Aquatic Ecosystems
The discharge of warm water after cooling processes can raise local water body temperatures, disrupting aquatic ecosystems by lowering oxygen levels and altering breeding cycles. Thermal pollution can lead to fish die-offs and changes in biodiversity near water bodies where data centres discharge heated water. Additionally, the use of chemical additives in cooling systems (to prevent microbial growth or corrosion) can introduce hazardous compounds into the environment if not properly treated.

  • Social & Regulatory Conflict
Data centres’ water use has sparked community resistance and legal battles, particularly when resource use is perceived as benefiting private corporations at public expense. In The Dalles, Oregon, a legal standoff emerged when Google’s water usage plan for its data centre became public, sparking public outcry and demands for transparency. In India, while regulations exist on groundwater use, enforcement is weak, and permits are sometimes granted without proper environmental assessments.

  

Sustainable Cooling Alternatives

With rising concerns around the environmental and water footprint of data centres, companies and researchers are increasingly investing in alternative cooling technologies that are both efficient and eco-friendly. Air Cooling and Free Cooling Techniques Air cooling, especially “free cooling”, uses ambient air instead of water or refrigerants, making it a water-efficient solution, particularly in cooler climates. Meta’s data centre in Luleå, Sweden, uses outside air for most of the year, reducing both water and energy usage. However, free cooling isn’t viable in hot or humid climates like much of India, which limits its global scalability. Liquid Immersion Cooling Instead of cooling the air around servers, liquid immersion cooling involves submerging server components in thermally conductive, non-electrically conductive liquids. This method can reduce water usage by 90–95% compared to traditional methods, while also lowering electricity costs. In India, startups and green-tech firms are piloting modular liquid-cooled server racks for high-density AI processing workloads. Direct-to-Chip (D2C) Liquid Cooling Another high-efficiency solution, D2C cooling, circulates water or special coolants directly over server chips using cold plates. Google and Meta are experimenting with D2C in their AI-focused data centres to reduce evaporation losses. Reuse of Waste Heat Many new-generation data centres are exploring district heating, where waste heat is reused to warm nearby buildings, greenhouses, or water systems. Amazon’s data centre in Dublin powers a district heating system that heats over 50,000 square meters of public and private infrastructure. This helps with reducing emissions, conserving water, and making energy usage more circular.

Water-Related Regulations and Policy Trends

 

While the U.S. has environmental laws governing water withdrawals and discharge, these don’t always apply cleanly to data centres. Many states do not require companies to disclose water usage publicly, especially if they use municipal water sources. In most U.S. jurisdictions, tech companies aren’t required to report how much water their data centres use. In 2021, residents of The Dalles discovered that Google's expansion would double its water use, prompting public outrage and calls for transparency. This led to legislation being proposed in New York to force disclosure of water use by large data centres.

 

India’s data centre sector is growing rapidly, but water use policies remain poorly enforced and fragmented across states. Most centres extract water through municipal o borewell connections, but monitoring is minimal. Water consumption in Indian data centres is rarely measured or reported, despite the country’s rising water stress. Gaps in Policy No mandatory disclosure of water consumption in the IT or cloud sector. Limited enforcement of groundwater usage restrictions, even in critical zones. No national policy linking data centre development to water conservation measures. In high-risk regions like Chennai and Hyderabad, data centres operate without a sustainable water strategy, raising long-term environmental and urban planning concerns.

 

Trends and Proposed Solutions

 
  • USA
    • Bills are being introduced to require public disclosure of water consumption.
    • Local governments (like Santa Clara, California) are limiting new data centre permits in water-stressed zones.
    • NGOs are pushing for water-use impact assessments during site approvals.
     
  • India
    • Proposals for Green Data Centre standards include water efficiency, but adoption is voluntary.
    • Some pilot projects are incorporating rainwater harvesting and treated wastewater for non-critical cooling.
    • The Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) is considering mandatory sustainability reporting for upcoming data centre parks.

    Comparative Analysis: Data Center Water Consumption - India vs. USA

     

    Overview

    Data centers in both India and the United States depend heavily on water for cooling, but there are significant differences in the scale of usage, technology adoption, regulatory frameworks, and geographic challenges related to water availability. These factors influence the environmental impact of data center operations in each country.

     

    Total water consumption

    In the United States, data centers collectively consume approximately 1.7 billion liters of water per day. On average, each kilowatt-hour of electricity consumed by a data center in the U.S. corresponds to 4.52 liters of water use. The country has over 2,700 large-scale data centers, making its water footprint significant. In India, total water use is currently lower but growing rapidly. The total volume consumed by data centers is expected to reach 150.3 billion liters in 2025 and is projected to increase to 358.7 billion liters by 2030, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of more than 19 percent. While the United States currently leads in overall water use due to the larger size of its data center industry, India's rate of increase is substantially higher. Without strong sustainability measures, India’s footprint may approach that of the United States in the coming years.

     

    Cooling Technologies

    U.S. data centers have traditionally relied on evaporative cooling, which is water-intensive. Although modern facilities are beginning to adopt closed-loop and liquid cooling systems to reduce water consumption, retrofitting existing infrastructure remains a challenge. In India, many new data centers are opting for air-cooled chillers and other dry cooling techniques from the outset. This is in part due to the country’s existing water stress in urban areas, which discourages the use of water-heavy technologies. India may be better positioned to implement water-efficient technologies at scale, as much of its infrastructure is still being built. In contrast, older U.S. facilities face limitations in modernizing quickly.

     

    Water Stress Regions

    In the United States, a significant number of data centers are located in states like Arizona, Utah, and California, which are already dealing with long-term water scarcity. In some regions, conflicts have emerged between municipal water needs and industrial users such as data centers. India faces similar pressures. Cities including Chennai, Hyderabad, and Bangalore already experience recurring water shortages and declining groundwater levels. These cities are also emerging hubs for digital infrastructure, increasing the risk of resource competition. Both countries face geographic vulnerabilities in terms of water availability. However, India’s higher population density intensifies the potential for disputes between public and industrial use.

     

    Policy and Regulation

    Regulation in the United States varies widely by state. Some states offer tax incentives or subsidies to data center operators without requiring strong environmental standards. However, recent legislative proposals, such as New York’s 2024 bill, aim to tie tax breaks and other benefits to sustainability metrics, including water use efficiency. India currently lacks a unified national policy on data center water consumption. Most practices are shaped by market forces or voluntary compliance with green building certifications, such as IGBC or LEED, which may incorporate water management benchmarks. While U.S. policy is starting to connect environmental performance with government support, India is still in the early stages of formulating such frameworks. The absence of mandatory water efficiency standards in India presents a regulatory gap.
